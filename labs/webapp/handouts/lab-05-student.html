1. Learning objectives

  1. Define radiographic, subject, and receptor contrast.
  2. Predict how kVp and tissue attenuation affect subject contrast.
  3. Compare long-scale and short-scale contrast images.
  4. Explain why digital processing can modify displayed contrast.
  5. Select a contrast strategy for a clinical imaging task.

2. Background concepts

Radiographic contrast is the visible difference between adjacent image regions. Subject contrast comes from differences in tissue attenuation; it is influenced by kVp, thickness, atomic number, and subject composition. Receptor contrast is the response characteristic of the receptor, while display processing can change the final appearance.

Short scale: few gray tones, higher visible contrast · Long scale: many gray tones, lower visible contrast. Lower kVp generally increases subject contrast, while higher kVp generally decreases it and increases penetration.

Contrast should be optimized for the diagnostic task, not maximized indiscriminately. The same raw signal can appear different when processing or windowing changes.

Simulation outputs are educational relative values, not calibrated dose or equipment specifications.

3. Experimental procedure

  1. Predict how increasing kVp changes subject contrast.
  2. Record a baseline run.
  3. Increase only kVp and record a second run.
  4. Restore kVp, change receptor/display processing, and record a third run.
  5. Separate subject-contrast changes from display-contrast changes.

Controlled-variable rule: after the baseline, change one variable at a time. Duplicate settings do not count as a new run.

4. Clinical challenge

A soft-tissue task requires subtle differences to remain visible. Which approach is most defensible?

Choose the response in the app, read the feedback, and be prepared to justify the decision using image quality, diagnostic value, or ALARA.

5. Worksheet guidance

  • 1. Compare two kVp settings and describe the change in subject contrast and penetration.
  • 2. Explain the difference between subject contrast and receptor contrast.
  • 3. Why can a digital image show useful contrast over a wider exposure range than film?
  • 4. Critical thinking: explain why “more contrast” is not always the best diagnostic choice.

Refer explicitly to run numbers and numerical results. A trend without evidence receives limited credit.
