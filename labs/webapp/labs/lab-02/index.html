Background: The Physics of Distance, Intensity & Geometry

Physical foundation of beam divergence, technique compensation, and magnification optics
Theory Primer

1.1 The Inverse-Square Law: Physical & Geometric Origin

X-rays are emitted isotropically from the tube focal spot and travel in straight divergent lines through space. Because the beam emerges from an effective point source, the area over which the photons spread increases as the square of the distance from the source ($Area \propto d^2$). Because the total number of photons emitted during an exposure is fixed by mAs, the photon fluence (photons per unit area, or radiation intensity $I$) decreases in inverse proportion to the square of the distance:

Inverse-Square Law Equation:
$$\frac{I_1}{I_2} = \left(\frac{d_2}{d_1}\right)^2 \iff I_2 = I_1 \times \left(\frac{d_1}{d_2}\right)^2$$
Where $I_1$ is the original intensity at distance $d_1$, and $I_2$ is the new intensity at distance $d_2$.
Clinical Consequence: If distance from the source doubles ($d_2 = 2 \cdot d_1$), beam intensity drops to one-quarter (25%) of its original value. If distance triples, intensity drops to one-ninth (~11.1%).

1.2 The Direct Square Law: Radiographic Technique Compensation

While the Inverse-Square Law describes what happens to radiation intensity when distance changes with no intervention, the radiographer must maintain a consistent, diagnostic detector exposure (and avoid quantum mottle) when clinical circumstances require changing the Source-to-Image Distance (SID). To keep the photon fluence reaching the image receptor constant, exposure quantity (mAs) must be adjusted in direct proportion to the square of the distance:

Direct Square Law (Exposure Maintenance Formula):
$$\frac{\text{mAs}_1}{\text{mAs}_2} = \left(\frac{d_1}{d_2}\right)^2 \iff \text{mAs}_2 = \text{mAs}_1 \times \left(\frac{d_2}{d_1}\right)^2$$
Diagnostic Example: A pelvis radiograph is calibrated at 10 mAs at 100 cm SID. If the SID must be increased to 140 cm, the required compensated mAs is:
$$\text{mAs}_2 = 10 \times \left(\frac{140}{100}\right)^2 = 10 \times (1.4)^2 = 10 \times 1.96 \approx 19.6 \approx 20\text{ mAs}$$
Clinical Rule of Thumb: Increasing SID from 100 cm to 140 cm requires approximately doubling the mAs; increasing SID from 100 cm to 180 cm (~72 inches) requires multiplying mAs by approximately 3.24×!

1.3 Geometric Optics: SID, SOD, OID & Magnification

Every radiographic exposure involves three fundamental geometric distances:

  • Source-to-Image Distance (SID): The total distance from the X-ray tube focal spot to the active surface of the image receptor.
  • Source-to-Object Distance (SOD): The distance from the focal spot to the anatomical structure of interest within the patient:
    $$\text{SOD} = \text{SID} - \text{OID}$$
  • Object-to-Image Distance (OID): The distance from the anatomical structure to the image receptor surface.

Because the X-ray beam diverges, any object positioned between the focal spot and receptor will project a shadow larger than its physical anatomical size. The degree of enlargement is defined by the Magnification Factor (MF):

Magnification Factor & Image Size Equations:
$$MF = \frac{\text{SID}}{\text{SOD}} = \frac{\text{SID}}{\text{SID} - \text{OID}}$$
$$\text{Image Size} = \text{Object Size} \times MF \quad \text{and} \quad \% \text{ Enlargement} = (MF - 1) \times 100\%$$
Geometric Blur (Penumbra): In addition to size enlargement, an elevated OID causes the finite focal spot size (FSS) to project an unsharp boundary called geometric blur or penumbra: $FGB = FSS \times OID / SOD$. To maximize spatial resolution and minimize distortion, the radiographer must always strive for the shortest practical OID and the longest practical SID.

1.4 Distance as Radiation Protection: Occupational ALARA

The three cardinal principles of radiation protection are Time, Distance, and Shielding. Among these, distance is the most practical immediate tool for staff during mobile and bedside work (shielding is still required where provided):

  • During diagnostic radiography, the patient acts as an extended source of Compton scattered radiation. Scatter intensity at 1 meter from the patient is approximately 0.1% (1/1000th) of the incident primary beam intensity for a typical field size at ~70 kVp (field- and kVp-dependent — see Bushong).
  • Applying the Inverse-Square Law to scatter: doubling your distance from the patient (e.g. stepping from 1 meter to 2 meters) reduces your scatter exposure by 75% (down to one-quarter). Stepping back to 3 meters reduces your exposure by nearly 89% (down to one-ninth).
  • In mobile (portable) radiography and C-arm fluoroscopy, where structural lead barriers are absent, maximizing distance from the patient and tube is paramount to maintaining occupational doses ALARA (As Low As Reasonably Achievable).

1.5 The Four Sequential Stages of Radiographic Image Formation

  1. Primary Beam Propagation: X-ray photons travel from the tube focal spot through filtration and collimator shutters. Intensity decreases strictly with distance squared ($I \propto 1/d^2$).
  2. Patient Interaction: The divergent beam enters the patient's body and is differentially attenuated via photoelectric absorption and Compton scattering, yielding remnant exit intensities.
  3. Image Capture: The remnant beam strikes the image receptor (DR flat panel, CR PSP, or screen-film), generating an electronic charge or latent image pattern.
  4. Image Processing & Display: Computerized ADC digitization, histogram analysis, Look-Up Table (LUT) mapping, and workstation monitor visualization.
