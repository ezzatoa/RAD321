Background Theory & Physical Foundations

Read thoroughly before beginning the interactive laboratory stations
Physics & Chemistry Primer

1.1 Cross-Sectional Anatomy of Double-Emulsion Film

Medical radiographic film is primarily duplitized (double-emulsion), featuring an identical emulsion layer coated on both sides of a central transparent base. This double coating doubles the radiation absorption efficiency, allowing a 50% reduction in patient exposure dose compared to single-emulsion film.

Surface Layer (~1 µm)

Supercoat / Overcoat

A thin, hardened gelatin layer that protects the delicate emulsion from mechanical scratches, abrasions, pressure marks, and darkroom chemical contamination.

Active Layer (~5–10 µm)

Double Emulsion

Gelatin suspension containing photosensitive silver halide microcrystals (95–98% $\text{AgBr}$, 2–5% $\text{AgI}$) with structural surface defects called sensitivity specks ($\text{Ag}_2\text{S}$).

Bonding Layer (~0.2 µm)

Adhesive / Subbing

A specialized gelatin bonding layer ensuring uniform, permanent adhesion between the hydrophilic emulsion and the hydrophobic polyester base.

Structural Support (~175 µm)

Polyester Base

Dimensionally stable, flexible, non-flammable polyethylene terephthalate tinted with a subtle blue dye to reduce diagnostic eyestrain on luminous viewboxes.

1.2 Intensifying Screen Physics & Spectral Matching

Less than 1% to 5% of film blackening is caused by direct X-ray photon absorption. Instead, the cassette houses two intensifying screens that absorb remnant X-ray photons and convert them into thousands of visible light photons through fluorescence. Over 95% to 99% of the radiographic image is recorded by visible light emitted from these intensifying screens, reducing patient radiation dose by a factor of 50 to 100.

Spectral Matching Principle:

The light emitted by the intensifying screen phosphor MUST match the spectral sensitivity of the radiographic film emulsion:

  • Green-Emitting Rare Earth Screens: Gadolinium oxysulfide ($\text{Gd}_2\text{O}_2\text{S:Tb}$, peak emission 545 nm) must be paired with Orthochromatic film (green + blue sensitive).
  • Blue/UV-Emitting Screens: Lanthanum oxybromide ($\text{LaOBr:Tm}$, peak emission 400 nm) or Calcium tungstate ($\text{CaWO}_4$) must be paired with Monochromatic film (blue/UV sensitive).
  • Spectral Mismatch Penalty: Mismatching green-emitting screens with blue-sensitive film causes an approx. 50% to 70% loss of system speed (classroom estimate — direction is what matters), forcing the radiographer to double or triple patient mAs to prevent underexposure!

1.3 Latent Image Formation: The Gurney-Mott Hypothesis

When remnant light and X-rays strike the silver halide crystal, an invisible electrochemical alteration occurs within the crystal lattice known as the latent image. The Gurney-Mott theory explains this process in 5 distinct stages:

  1. Photoelectron Ejection: An incoming light or X-ray photon is absorbed by a bromide ion ($\text{Br}^-$), releasing a high-speed photoelectron into the crystal conduction band: $$\text{Br}^- + h\nu \longrightarrow \text{Br}^0 + e^-$$ The neutral bromine atom ($\text{Br}^0$) diffuses away into the surrounding gelatin matrix.
  2. Electron Trapping: The free conduction electron migrates through the crystal lattice until it becomes trapped at a sensitivity speck (silver sulfide defect, $\text{Ag}_2\text{S}$) on the crystal surface, imparting a temporary negative electrostatic charge to the speck.
  3. Interstitial Silver Ion Migration: Mobile, positively charged interstitial silver ions ($\text{Ag}^+$) within the crystal lattice are electrostatically drawn toward the negatively charged sensitivity speck.
  4. Neutral Silver Atom Deposition: Upon reaching the speck, the silver ion neutralizes the trapped electron, precipitating a single atom of metallic black silver: $$\text{Ag}^+ + e^- \longrightarrow \text{Ag}^0$$
  5. Latent Image Center Formation: As successive photons interact with the crystal, this cycle repeats. When a sensitivity speck accumulates a cluster of 3 or more metallic silver atoms ($\text{Ag}_n^0,\ n \ge 3$), it forms a stable latent image center capable of catalyzing full chemical reduction in the developer tank.

1.4 Darkroom Environmental & Safelight Physics

The darkroom must be completely light-tight, utilizing a maze labyrinth or double-door interlock. Working illumination is provided by specialized safelights designed to deliver visible illumination without exposing the photosensitive film emulsion:

Safelight Specifications & Inverse-Square Protection:
  • Filter Transmission:
    • Kodak GBX-2 (Ruby Red): Transmits wavelengths $\lambda > 600\text{ nm}$. Safe for both orthochromatic (green) and monochromatic (blue) films.
    • Kodak Wratten 6B (Amber): Transmits wavelengths $\lambda > 560\text{ nm}$. Safe only for monochromatic (blue) films. Will rapidly fog orthochromatic film!
  • Bulb Wattage & Minimum Distance: Maximum 15-watt frosted bulb positioned at a minimum distance of 1.2 meters (120 cm / 4 feet) from workbenches. Under the inverse-square law, reducing distance from 120 cm to 60 cm quadruples light intensity ($4\times$), causing severe darkroom post-exposure fog.
  • Relative Humidity (RH): Must be maintained between 40% and 60%. Low humidity ($< 35\%$) promotes electrostatic discharges, creating permanent tree, crown, or smudge static artifacts. High humidity ($> 60\%$) causes emulsion swelling, roller slippage, and transport jamming.

1.5 Automatic Processor Chemistry (5 Stages)

In 90-second rapid automatic processing, the film travels sequentially through 5 dedicated functional stages:

  1. Developer Tank (Alkaline pH ~10.0–10.5, 35°C / 95°F, ~22s): Converts latent image centers into visible metallic black silver grains ($\text{Ag}^0$). Uses a synergistic combination of Phenidone (fast-acting reducer, develops gray mid-tones) and Hydroquinone (slow-acting reducer, builds heavy black densities and high contrast). Contains potassium bromide as a restrainer to prevent chemical fog, sodium sulfite as an antioxidant preservative, and glutaraldehyde as a gelatin hardener.
    Classroom Activity Model (simplified temperature rule, not the full Arrhenius law):
    Developer chemical activity doubles approximately every 5°C elevation above baseline: $$\text{Activity} = 2^{\frac{T - 35}{5}} \times \left(\frac{t}{90}\right)$$ Where $T$ is developer temperature (°C) and $t$ is total processing immersion time (seconds). Overheating causes rapid chemical fog and loss of diagnostic contrast!
  2. Stop / Squeegee Rollers: Squeegee roller racks compress the film, removing excess alkaline developer fluid to prevent chemical carryover and neutralizer exhaustion in the fixer.
  3. Fixer Tank (Acidic pH ~4.0–4.5, ~35°C, ~20s): Halts all development immediately with acetic acid neutralizer. Contains Ammonium thiosulfate ("hypo"), which dissolves unexposed, undeveloped silver halide crystals into soluble silver thiosulfate complexes: $$\text{AgBr} + 2(\text{NH}_4)_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_3 \longrightarrow (\text{NH}_4)_3[\text{Ag}(\text{S}_2\text{O}_3)_2] + \text{NH}_4\text{Br}$$ Also contains potassium alum to harden and stiffen the gelatin for roller transport.
  4. Wash Tank (Fresh Running Water, 30°C–35°C, ~20s): Removes residual ammonium thiosulfate and dissolved silver complexes. Inadequate washing leaves residual hypo, causing the radiograph to turn brown/yellow over time due to silver sulfide formation.
  5. Dryer (Warm Forced Air, 45°C–55°C, ~20s): Evaporates moisture from the swollen gelatin, producing a permanent, scratch-resistant, diagnostically stable radiograph.

Mechanical Contact

Radiation Shield

6. Lead Foil Backing

Thin sheet of radiopaque lead foil ($Z=82$) lining the rear lid, absorbing backscattered radiation from the Bucky tray/wall stand to eliminate foggy image degradation.
