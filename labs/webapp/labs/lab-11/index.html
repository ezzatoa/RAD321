Background Theory Primer

Physical conversion pathways, active matrix arrays, and spatial sampling mathematics
Physics Primer

1.1 Digital Receptor Classifications & Conversion Pathways

Digital radiography has replaced analog film-screen systems by converting incident X-ray photon patterns into discrete digital matrices. Modern clinical radiology utilizes three distinct receptor architectures:

  1. Computed Radiography (CR / Photostimulable Phosphor): Uses a flexible cassette containing barium fluorohalide doped with europium ($\text{BaFBr:Eu}^{2+}$). X-ray photons ionize europium atoms, trapping photoelectrons in metastable crystal lattice energy bands (F-centers). In the CR reader, a focused red helium-neon laser ($633\text{ nm}$; modern readers use 660–690 nm diodes) stimulates the trapped electrons to release blue-violet photostimulated luminescence ($\text{PSL}, 390\text{ nm}$), which is collected by a light guide and amplified by a photomultiplier tube (PMT). Turbid crystal scattering during laser readout creates optical diffusion blur that degrades high-frequency Modulation Transfer Function (MTF).
  2. Indirect Conversion Flat-Panel DR (Scintillator + Photodiode TFT): Uses a structured needle-like Cesium Iodide ($\text{CsI:Tl}$) scintillator screen optically coupled to an active matrix array (AMA) of amorphous silicon ($\text{a-Si}$) photodiodes. Incident X-rays generate visible light photons that channel along the needle crystals to minimize lateral light spread, which the photodiodes convert into proportional electric charge stored in thin-film transistor (TFT) capacitors.
  3. Direct Conversion Flat-Panel DR (Photoconductor + High-Voltage TFT): Eliminates the optical intermediate completely by using an amorphous Selenium ($\text{a-Se}$) photoconductor layer under a high-voltage electrostatic bias field ($5–10\text{ kV/mm}$, i.e. field strength ~10 V/µm). Absorbed X-rays directly produce electron-hole pairs that travel strictly parallel to the vertical electric field lines. With zero lateral light scatter, direct DR provides the highest Modulation Transfer Function (MTF) and sharpness, making it the gold standard for microcalcification detection in digital mammography.

1.2 Matrix Sampling Mathematics & Effective Pixel Pitch

In digital imaging, spatial fidelity is governed by two physical sampling apertures: the physical detector element (DEL) spacing on the flat panel and the digital matrix dimensions across the active Field of View (FOV):

$$\text{Matrix Pixel Size: } p_{\text{matrix}} = \frac{\text{FOV (mm)}}{\text{Matrix Size}} = \frac{\text{FOV (cm)} \times 10}{\text{Matrix Size}}$$ $$\text{Effective Sampling Pitch: } p_{\text{eff}} = \max(p_{\text{DEL}}, p_{\text{matrix}})$$ $$\text{Sampling Frequency: } f_s = \frac{1}{p_{\text{eff}}}\text{ (samples/mm)}$$

Sampling Bottlenecks: If $p_{\text{matrix}} > p_{\text{DEL}}$, the system is Matrix-Limited (pixelation blurs detector detail; increasing matrix size restores resolution). If $p_{\text{DEL}} > p_{\text{matrix}}$, the system is Detector-Limited (physical DEL spacing bottlenecks detail; increasing matrix provides empty digital magnification without new anatomical data).

1.3 The Shannon-Nyquist Sampling Theorem & Moiré Aliasing

To faithfully record a high-frequency anatomical line-pair without distortion, the imaging system must acquire at least two sample points per cycle. The maximum spatial frequency that a digital system can record without artifact is the Nyquist Limiting Frequency ($f_N$):

$$\text{Nyquist Limiting Frequency: } f_N = \frac{f_s}{2} = \frac{1}{2 \cdot p_{\text{eff}}}\text{ (lp/mm)}$$ $$\text{Moiré Aliasing Frequency: } f_{\text{alias}} = |f_s - f_{\text{input}}| \quad (\text{when } f_{\text{input}} > f_N)$$

Simulator form shown; general form is |f − n·fs| folded into ±fN.

Aliasing Mechanics: When fine anatomical structures or anti-scatter grid lead strips possess a spatial frequency exceeding $f_N$, the detector cannot capture the peaks and troughs correctly. The unresolvable high-frequency signal wraps back (folds) across the Nyquist threshold, manifesting as a false, low-frequency wave pattern known as a Moiré artifact or aliasing.

1.4 DEL Fill Factor & Limiting Spatial Resolution

Each square detector element (DEL) on an active matrix array contains an active radiation-sensitive sensing area, plus dead space occupied by the TFT switch, storage capacitor, and row/column gate lines:

$$\text{DEL Fill Factor} = \frac{\text{Active Sensing Area}}{\text{Total DEL Area}} \times 100\%$$ $$\text{Limiting Spatial Resolution (10\% MTF): } R_{10\%} \approx f_N \times \text{MTF}_{\text{material}}$$ $$\text{Minimum Resolvable Feature Width: } \Delta x = \frac{1}{2 \cdot R_{10\%}}\text{ (mm)}$$

Simulator proxy: true R10% is the frequency where MTF = 10% on a measured curve; Δx = 1/(2·R10%) is model-specific.

The Fill Factor Penalty: Because TFT readout electronics have fixed physical dimensions, reducing DEL pitch (e.g., from $200\text{ \mu m}$ down to $80\text{ \mu m}$) causes the fill factor to drop (from $\sim 85\%$ down to $\sim 55\%$). A lower fill factor reduces overall detector quantum efficiency, requiring higher patient radiation dose to maintain equivalent signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)!

Technology 1 Computed Radiography (CR / PSP)

Barium fluorohalide crystals ($\text{BaFBr:Eu}^{2+}$) trap ionized electrons in crystal vacancies. Reading requires mechanical feeding into a scanner where a red laser stimulates blue-violet luminescence. Turbid crystal scattering during laser transit degrades MTF to $\sim 70\%$ at Nyquist (illustrative; clinical ~0.1–0.3).

Conversion: X-ray $\to$ trapped electron energy $\to$ laser stimulated light $\to$ PMT analog voltage.

Technology 2 Indirect Conversion Flat-Panel DR

Structured needle-like Cesium Iodide ($\text{CsI:Tl}$) crystals channel emitted scintillation light directly down to an active matrix array of amorphous silicon ($\text{a-Si}$) photodiodes. Light channeling minimizes lateral diffusion, maintaining $\sim 85\%$ MTF at Nyquist (illustrative; clinical ~0.1–0.3) with high absorption efficiency.

Conversion: X-ray $\to$ channeled visible light $\to$ photodiode electric charge $\to$ TFT readout.

Technology 3 Direct Conversion Flat-Panel DR

Amorphous Selenium ($\text{a-Se}$) photoconductor under $+5\text{ kV}$ bias field. X-rays create electron-hole pairs collected along vertical electric field lines. With zero lateral light spread, direct DR achieves $\sim 95\%$ MTF (illustrative; clinical ~0.1–0.3), maximizing micro-calcification sharpness.

Conversion: X-ray $\to$ direct electron-hole pairs $\to$ vertical electrostatic collection $\to$ TFT readout.

Technology 4 Active Matrix Array (AMA) & Fill Factor

Grid of detector elements (DELs) etched on glass substrates. Each DEL contains a sensing area, TFT switch, and capacitor. The ratio of active sensing area to total area defines the Fill Factor. Smaller DELs increase Nyquist resolution but sacrifice fill factor and dose efficiency.

Trade-off: Smaller DEL pitch ($80\text{ \mu m}$) improves lp/mm but drops fill factor to $55\%$, raising dose.
