Background: How Is a Radiographic Image Made?

Physical foundation of X-ray production, tube thermodynamics, and the imaging chain
Theory Primer

1.1 What Is a Radiograph?

A radiograph is a two-dimensional shadowgram created when an X-ray beam traverses a patient's body and is intercepted by an image receptor. Different biological tissues absorb and scatter (attenuate) the beam in varying proportions. Dense, high-atomic-number structures (such as cortical bone, $Z \approx 13.8$) undergo high photoelectric absorption, allowing few photons to reach the receptor beneath them, producing a light (radiopaque) area. Conversely, low-density tissues (such as air-filled lung parenchyma) attenuate few photons, allowing a high photon fluence to expose the detector, producing a dark (radiolucent) area. This differential pattern of exit intensities constitutes the diagnostic image.

1.2 The X-ray Tube: Where the Beam Begins

Every radiographic exposure begins inside an evacuated glass or metal X-ray tube envelope immersed in insulating transformer oil within a protective lead housing. Two primary electrodes establish the high-voltage circuit:

  • Cathode (–): Contains a coiled tungsten filament ($W$, atomic number $Z=74$, melting point $3422^\circ\text{C}$). When heated by a dedicated low-voltage filament current (3.8–4.8 A), it emits a cloud of free electrons via thermionic emission. The negatively charged nickel focusing cup electrostatically repels the electrons, focusing them into a narrow stream toward the focal track.
  • Anode (+): A rotating molybdenum-graphite disc with a tungsten-rhenium focal track ($Z=74$). Accelerated by the high tube potential (kVp), electrons bombard the focal track at relativistic speeds. Rotation (3,000–10,000 RPM) spreads the intense focal heat over an expanded circular track area.
Bremsstrahlung Production Efficiency Equation:
$$\eta \approx 10^{-9} \times Z \times V$$
Where $Z$ represents the anode target atomic number (Tungsten = 74, Molybdenum = 42) and $V$ represents the accelerating potential in Volts ($kVp \times 1000$).
Diagnostic Example at 80 kVp with Tungsten:
$$\eta \approx 10^{-9} \times 74 \times 80,000 = 0.00592 \approx 0.59\%$$
Thermodynamic Reality: Over 99.4% of electron kinetic energy converts directly into thermal heat via outer-shell coulombic collisions; less than 0.6% yields Bremsstrahlung X-ray photons!
Teaching simplification (Bushong/Carlton use $\eta \approx 1.1 \times 10^{-9} \cdot Z \cdot V$, max ≈1% in diagnosis). Heat comes mostly from excitation/ionization of outer-shell electrons; <1% leaves as Bremsstrahlung + characteristic X-rays.

The useful X-ray beam exits through a thin tube window. Inherent filtration from the envelope and oil, combined with specified aluminum added filtration, removes low-energy ("soft") photons that lack the penetration to reach the receptor and would only deposit useless radiation dose in the patient's skin. NCRP Report 102 mandates a minimum of 2.5 mm Al equivalent total filtration for systems operating above 70 kVp (still the US federal requirement under 21 CFR; see also NCRP 147 and local regulations). Downstream, the light-beam collimator uses adjustable lead shutters to restrict the field size strictly to the anatomical region of interest, significantly reducing both patient dose and Compton scatter.

1.3 The Four Stages of Image Intensity Formation

Every radiographic image undergoes four sequential stages from exposure initiation to clinical interpretation:

  1. Primary Beam Propagation: X-rays travel from the target through filtration and collimator shutters to the patient's skin surface. Intensity is governed by kVp, mA, exposure time, filtration, and source-to-image distance (SID).
  2. Patient Interaction: The beam is differentially attenuated as it traverses tissue through photoelectric absorption and Compton scattering, emerging as the modulated remnant (exit) beam.
  3. Image Capture: The remnant beam strikes the active layer of the image receptor (Screen-Film phosphor, CR photostimulable phosphor, DR flat-panel scintillator/photodiode array, or Fluoroscopy input phosphor).
  4. Image Processing & Display: The latent energy pattern is converted into a visible radiograph — through chemical processing for film, or computerized ADC sampling, histogram rescaling, and Look-Up Table (LUT) mapping for digital systems.

1.4 The Radiographer's Two Interconnected Roles

Optimal Image Recording

Pre-exposure responsibilities: patient positioning, SID alignment, radiation shielding, collimation to anatomy of interest, receptor selection, and technical factor optimization (kVp, mA, time, filtration) to achieve diagnostic photon statistics while honoring ALARA.

Optimal Image Analysis

Post-exposure responsibilities: rigorous image critique, verifying anatomical inclusion without cutoff, assessing exposure index (EI/S-number), evaluating spatial resolution, detecting quantum mottle or noise, and determining whether repeat imaging is clinically necessary.
